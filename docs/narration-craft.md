# Writing for spoken narration

Narrate turns written material into a clear spoken script. A good narration
keeps the source's meaning and level of certainty while making the language
comfortable to hear once, without rereading.

## Keep the source intact

- Preserve the source's claims, qualifications, sequence, and important
  distinctions.
- Keep names, numbers, dates, and technical terms accurate. Explain a term
  briefly when the listener needs it.
- Do not fill gaps with invented facts, examples, quotations, motives, or
  personal stories. If the source is uncertain, keep that uncertainty clear.
- Retain disagreement or tension when it matters to the point.

## Make it easy to follow by ear

- Use direct sentences, familiar words, and natural contractions where they fit.
- Give the listener signposts when the subject or reasoning changes.
- Prefer a concrete, source-supported example over an abstract restatement.
- Break dense lists into groups and explain why each group matters.
- Vary sentence length and allow room around complex ideas. Avoid repeated
  summaries and decorative scene-setting.
- Use a warm, respectful tone without pretending to have met, researched, or
  interviewed anyone.

## Match the material

Keep the source's purpose and audience in view. A technical explanation should
stay precise; a personal account should retain its voice; a set of instructions
should make the next action easy to hear. Use humor, emotional emphasis, and
an opening hook only when the material supports them.

## Review before speaking

Check the script against the source for changed meaning, missing caveats,
mispronounced names or terms, unsupported detail, awkward transitions, and
sentences that are difficult to understand on first hearing. Automated
transcription comparisons can help locate differences, but they do not replace
checking the source or listening to the recording.
